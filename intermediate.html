The Mate's Handbook

From rules to reasons — the bridge between playing your cards and playing the game
The ship's library: I. The Deckhand's Manual · II. The Mate's Handbook · III. The Wardroom Primer

The deckhand's lessons — board reading, position, the pot's price, outs, and the discipline not to pay people off — will stop you losing. This book is about winning, and it begins with a promotion of perspective. The deckhand plays his cards. The mate plays the situation: what all of an opponent's possible hands look like, what share of the pot each side's collection truly owns, and — above everything — why a bet is being made. Learn the why and the game turns transparent; skip it and you can memorize moves forever and never know which one to reach for.

I. He doesn't have a hand either — he has a range

The single idea that separates the middle of poker from the bottom.

You will never know your opponent's two cards, and the wonderful news is that you don't need to. What you can know — from how he's played the hand, and how he plays generally — is the collection of hands he could hold: his range. He raised before the flop? The 9-4s and J-2s leave the collection. He called a bet on an ace-high flop? The pure air thins out; pairs and draws remain. Every action is a sieve, and by the river, a player who was “anything” has become “one of a few dozen combinations.”

Thinking in ranges rescues you from poker's oldest torment: “what does he have?” Wrong question — unanswerable, and the guess swings on mood. The mate's question is “what does he have mostly?” — and that one has an answer you can act on. When you catch yourself putting him on exactly ace-king and playing accordingly, you've slipped back to the deck. Climb up: he has a weighted collection, and you're playing against its average.

Play the range, not the guess.

II. Equity: your true share of the pot

Every pot already belongs to both of you, in proportion.

Put your hand against his range and run every future card: some fraction of the time you finish best. That fraction is your equity — your rightful share of the pot if all hands were simply dealt to the end. A flush draw against top pair holds about 35%; a pair against two overcards is the famous coin flip; top pair against a worse top pair is a landslide.

Equity turns the deckhand's pot-odds arithmetic into a complete engine. The price says what winning chance you need; equity says what you hold; call when you hold more than you need. But its deeper use is this: equity explains why the money goes in now rather than later. Betting with 65% equity isn't about the hands you fold out — every chip that enters the pot while you're the 65% is a chip you own 65 cents of on arrival. Strong hands bet partly to be paid — and partly because building the pot while ahead is itself the profit.

III. The two reasons to bet

There are only two. Everything else is superstition.

Ask a table of beginners why they bet and you'll hear a folk taxonomy: “to protect my hand,” “to find out where I stand,” “to take control.” Here is the mate's full and complete list of reasons a bet makes money:

  • Value — a worse hand calls. Your equity when called exceeds your share of the price; the call pays you.
  • Bluff — a better hand folds. Equity that belonged to him is surrendered to you.

That's the list. “Information” is what value bets and bluffs produce as exhaust, not a reason on its own — a bet made only to find out where you stand buys the answer at full price and does nothing with it. “Protection” is real but it is simply value's shadow — charging worse hands that might improve is a value bet. Before every bet you ever make, name which of the two you're doing: which worse hands call me, or which better hands fold? If you can name neither, you've found the third kind of bet — the donation — and the correct alternative was standing quietly still.

Every bet is a claim: worse hands call, or better hands fold. If you can't finish the sentence, don't make the bet.

IV. The board has moods

Texture: whom a flop befriends, and how fast the friendship expires.

Boards come in temperaments. K♠ 7♦ 2♣ is dry — no draws, nothing coordinated; whoever was ahead on it will usually still be ahead on the river, and hands change value slowly. 9♥ 8♥ 7♠ is wet — straights live, flushes loom, every turn card rearranges the standings. Texture sets the tempo of correct play:

  • On dry boards, made hands can relax — small bets, patient lines, little fear of the next card. Bluffs need to be chosen well, because nothing scary will arrive to help them.
  • On wet boards, made hands are perishable goods — value bets grow urgent (charge the draws while you're still ahead), and calls with one modest pair grow worse (too many rivers beat you).
  • And ask of every flop: whose range does it befriend? An ace-high flop loves the preflop raiser; a middling connected flop loves the caller. The befriended range gets to bet more often — sometimes regardless of its actual two cards.

V. The check is not weakness

Medium hands earn by standing still.

The deckhand learned not to pay people off. The mate learns the fuller truth that was hiding underneath: hands in the middle of your range — decent pairs, modest tops — earn their money passively. Bet them and watch chapter III collapse: no worse hand calls, no better hand folds. Check them, and something better happens — opponents holding the air you couldn't get paid by start bluffing at you, and their bluffs become your value. A medium hand that checks and calls is called a bluff-catcher, and deploying it on purpose — rather than betting it out of nerves — is the most reliable single upgrade at this stage of the game.

Notice what this does to how your whole range plays: your strong hands bet, your hopeless hands sometimes bluff, and your middle stands guard. Each family has a job. That division of labor is the actual content of the word “strategy.”

VI. Aggression with a future: the semi-bluff

The happiest bet in poker profits twice.

Now combine equity with the bluff. Bet a flush draw and count your ways to win: he folds — the pot is yours immediately, with the worst hand. He calls — a third of the deck still crowns you, and the pot you'd win got bigger while you had the fold equity as a cushion. This is the semi-bluff, and it is why strong players seem so much more aggressive than the deckhand thinks is safe: their “bluffs” are rarely hopeless. They are bets that win two ways, made with hands that were going to play anyway.

The mate's rule for choosing them: aggression wants equity or fold equity, ideally both. A draw bets happily. Pure air bluffs only where the story adds up. And the middle — say it once more — stands still.

VII. The same spot has more than one right answer

A first look over the bridge, at what Book Three holds.

One more promotion of perspective and the handbook is done. The deckhand wants a rulebook: this hand, this board, do this. But watch what happens when a good player faces the same situation twice — sometimes a different action comes out. Not indecision: frequencies. If you bet every strong hand and checked every weak one, your bets would be a signed confession; opponents could fold exactly when you have it and attack exactly when you don't. Skilled play mixes — mostly this action, sometimes that one — precisely so that no single action gives the hand away.

Here is the astonishing part, and the door this book leaves you standing before: for the game this room trains — heads-up fixed-limit hold'em — those frequencies have been computed. The game is solved. There is a strategy that cannot be beaten in the long run, it plays at this table, and when you press the hint it tells you its actual numbers — check 98%, raise 46% — for the exact spot you're in. Book Three is a tour of that solution's mind: why the middle checks, why junk makes the best bluffs, why seven-high sometimes calls a river bet, and how a meter can measure — in big blinds, exactly — how far from perfect your evening was.

VIII. The mate's practice regimen

Turning chapters into reflexes.

  • Play heads-up (⚔), casual, coach off. Real decisions first; the chart's opinion after. Before each bet, finish chapter III's sentence aloud: worse calls, or better folds?
  • Turn on the leak chip 🎯 (key 5) — it scores your river decisions against the exact solution, in bb/100, live. Under 5 is the historical pro bar; watch it as a trend, not a verdict.
  • After each match, open the scorecard and replay the red rows. Both hands face up. For every mistake, ask the range question: what was his collection, and which family was my hand in — value, bluff, or bluff-catcher? Miscategorized family is the intermediate leak; the replays will show you yours.
  • The 📋 copy button puts any hand — actions, chart mixes, exact EV losses — on your clipboard as text. Argue with a friend or an AI; the numbers referee.

That's the bridge crossed: ranges instead of guesses, equity instead of hope, two honest reasons to bet, boards with moods, a middle that stands guard, aggression that profits twice, and frequencies instead of a rulebook. You are no longer playing your cards; you are playing the situation. What remains is to meet the strategy that plays the situation perfectly — and to start measuring your distance from it.
