The Deckhand's Manual

Poker's first real lessons, for the player who knows the rules and nothing else
The ship's library: I. The Deckhand's Manual · II. The Mate's Handbook · III. The Wardroom Primer

You know what beats what. You know the blinds go in, the cards come, and the betting travels around. That makes you a deckhand: aboard, useful, and — until this book is done — a source of steady income for everyone else at the table. The rules of poker are five minutes; the game hides behind them. This manual holds the six lessons that get you from knowing the rules to no longer being the mark, and every one of them can be practiced tonight against the ship's own characters, in a room that will tell you honestly how it's going.

I. You don't have a hand — you have a hand on a board

Strength is relative, and the board sets the exchange rate.

The rankings chart says a pair beats high card and two pair beats one. True, and nearly useless. The first real lesson is that the same holding is a monster on one board and a liability on another. A pair of tens is delightful on 10♠ 6♦ 2♣ — top pair, nothing sensible ahead of it. The same tens on A♠ K♠ Q♥ are a mess: one pair, four kinds of better pair in easy reach, straights already possible. Nothing about your cards changed. The board changed what they mean.

So train this reflex before any other: when the flop lands, don't look at your hand and ask “what do I have?” Look at the board and ask two questions —

  • What's the best hand my cards make with these five? (Beginners routinely misread — count it out.)
  • What better hands does this board offer someone else? Pairs above mine? A flush possible? A straight already there?

Top pair on a quiet board is treasure. Third pair on a coordinated board is a polite way to lose money. Learn to tell them apart at a glance and you've passed the first watch.

II. Fold. Yes, again.

Patience is not a virtue here; it is a weapon.

The most profitable button on the table is the red one. Beginners lose most of their money before the flop, by paying to see it with cards that were never going anywhere — the ragged aces, the pretty-looking 9-4 suited, the “any two can win” superstition. Any two can win. Most two don't, and they cost money the whole way to finding out.

Here is the honest arithmetic of it: when you start with the better hand, everything afterward is easier — your top pairs have better kickers, your two pairs are higher, your coin flips are weighted. Folding weak starts isn't timid; it is choosing to fight only where you outgun. In the six-handed game, that means folding most of what you're dealt without regret. (Heads-up — this room's specialty — you'll play far looser, because with only two hands dealt, mediocre cards are often the best cards. The principle survives: play the hands that figure to be ahead of the opponent's, and that depends on how many opponents there are.)

Every chip you don't lose is a chip won. The fold is the only bet that never loses.

III. Position is money

Acting last is a permanent, legal advantage.

In every betting round, someone must act first and someone gets to act last — and the player acting last makes every decision knowing what the other did. They check to you? You may take the pot with a bet. They bet? You fold your junk having lost nothing extra. The player acting last simply receives more information before every choice, on every street, for the whole hand — and in a game of information, that compounds into real money.

The practical rules a deckhand can apply tonight:

  • Play more hands when you'll act last (on or near the button), fewer when you'll act first.
  • Out of position, prefer strong, simple hands — the ones that don't need delicate judgment on every street, because you'll be making those judgments half-blind.
  • When you're unsure whether a marginal hand is playable, let position break the tie: last says play, first says fold.

IV. The pot lays you a price

Every call is a purchase. Learn to read the tag.

You face a bet of 40, and the pot — everything already in the middle, including that bet — is 200. The table is offering you a deal: pay 40 for a chance at 200. Five to one. That means the call doesn't need to win half the time to be right. It needs to win one time in six.

The price, in one line
call ÷ (pot + call) = the winning chance you need. 40 ÷ 240 = 1 in 6 ≈ 17%.

This single fraction converts poker from vibes to arithmetic. A hand worth abandoning against a huge bet is often worth keeping against a small one — not because the hand changed, but because the price did. From today, never call by feel. Glance at the pot, glance at the bet, and know the number you're being asked to beat.

V. Outs, and the deck's arithmetic

Hope, counted.

When you don't yet have the best hand, your chance of getting there is countable. Cards that improve you to the winner are outs. A flush draw after the flop: thirteen of your suit, minus the four you can see, is nine outs. An open-ended straight draw: eight. A gutshot: four.

And the deckhand's rule of thumb, accurate enough for a lifetime: outs × 2 ≈ your percentage per coming card (× 4 if two cards are still to come). Nine outs on the flop ≈ 36% to get there by the river; one card to come ≈ 18%.

Now marry it to the last chapter and you're doing real poker: the pot offers 5-to-1 (need 17%), you hold a flush draw at 18% for the next card — the call is correct, and no feeling was consulted at any point. Two numbers, one comparison. That comparison, made honestly on every street, is most of what “good” means at this stage.

VI. Don't pay people off

The single most expensive habit in beginner poker.

Here is where beginners' money actually goes. It isn't the wild bluffs — beginners don't bluff much. It goes like this: they make a decent-but-modest hand — second pair, a weak top pair — someone starts betting at them, betting bigger, raising — and they call, and call, and call, muttering “I have to see it,” all the way to the truth. The truth was available earlier, at a discount.

When quiet opponents suddenly play loud — when the bets grow street after street into a big pot — a modest made hand shrinks. Not to zero: sometimes they're bluffing, and the pot's price can justify one look. But repeatedly paying big bets with one modest pair is the leak that funds everyone else's evening. The discipline is unglamorous and worth more than any fancy move in this book:

Big pots are for big hands. When the pot grows loud and yours is modest, believe people.

VII. Practicing in this room

The instruments, set for training wheels.

The Wardroom is built for exactly this stage of the voyage. A practical setup for a deckhand's session:

  • Start at the six-handed table against the ship's five characters — they play their natures (the rock, the maniac, the calling station), and learning to name those natures is a lesson this manual couldn't fit.
  • Switch to heads-up (⚔) with the 💡 hint when curious — one press shows how the trained strategy would mix the decision you're facing. Use it after deciding, as a check, not before, as a crutch.
  • Keys: 1 fold, 2 check/call, 3 bet/raise. The chart room 📋 keeps your report card.

And a session ritual worth adopting from day one: after each match, open the scorecard on the trophy screen and click the hands that cost you. Both hands shown, every action annotated. You will discover — everyone discovers — that the losses cluster in chapters V and VI: prices misread, people paid off. That discovery, made with your own hands as the evidence, is this whole manual taking hold.

That's the deckhand's watch: read the board, fold without grief, sit where you act last, read the price, count your outs, and keep your chips out of other people's big hands. None of it is clever. All of it is money. When these six feel less like rules and more like reflexes, you're ready for the officer's track — where hands stop being cards and become ranges.
